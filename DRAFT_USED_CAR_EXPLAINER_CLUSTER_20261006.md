# Used-car buying explainers: listing red flags, price ratings, and total cost of ownership

**Status: proposed, pending André confirmation. Not for publication.**  
**Author: André Broekman**  
**Editorial scope:** Three question-led explainers for the GetCarWise education cluster. Each section can stand alone as a post or be adapted into a related guide after editorial review.

## 1. What are the red flags in a used-car listing?

A used-car listing deserves extra scrutiny when key details are missing, the seller will not provide the VIN or documents, the price or condition cannot be verified, or the seller pressures you to pay before seeing the car. None of these signs proves fraud by itself. Pause, verify the facts, and do not let urgency replace an inspection.

### What information should a credible listing include?

Look for enough detail to identify the exact vehicle: year, make, model, trim, mileage, VIN, condition, title status, price, seller identity, and clear photos of the actual car. A listing that omits details may be incomplete rather than dishonest, but you should ask the seller to fill the gaps before traveling or sending money.

Compare the VIN in the listing with the VIN on the vehicle and its documents. Ask whether the car has a clean or branded title and request supporting paperwork. For a dealer sale, review the FTC Buyers Guide, which indicates whether the vehicle is sold with a warranty or “as is,” subject to applicable law. The Guide does not replace the purchase contract; read both.

Sources: [FTC: Buying a Used Car From a Dealer](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-used-car-dealer) and [FTC Buyers Guide overview](https://consumer.ftc.gov/media/79915).

### Is a very low advertised price proof that a listing is a scam?

No. A low price can have legitimate explanations, including condition, mileage, equipment, location, title history, or a seller’s circumstances. But a price that appears far below comparable vehicles is a reason to check the listing carefully, confirm the full price and required fees in writing, and avoid paying a deposit before you verify the seller and vehicle.

Search for comparable examples with similar year, trim, mileage, condition, and location. A marketplace “deal” label can help identify a price worth investigating, but pricing methods differ across services and an estimate is not a binding offer. Ask whether the displayed price includes mandatory dealer fees and optional products. The FTC advises shoppers to focus on the total price and review the contract before signing.

Source: [FTC: Buying a Used Car From a Dealer](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-used-car-dealer).

### What seller behavior should make me pause?

Be cautious if the seller refuses an independent inspection, will not let you see the vehicle or its title documents, asks you to use a specific paid history-report site, or pushes for a fast deposit through an unusual payment method. These behaviors are reasons to stop and verify, not a substitute for evidence about the seller.

The FTC warns about vehicle-history-report scams in which a supposed buyer directs a seller to a particular report site. Buyers should choose a reputable report source themselves rather than following an unfamiliar payment link. For an actual purchase, request a history report and have the car inspected by an independent mechanic. A history report does not replace an inspection.

Sources: [FTC: Steering Clear of Vehicle History Report Scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2018/10/steering-clear-vehicle-history-report-scams) and [FTC: Buying a Used Car From a Dealer](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-used-car-dealer).

### Can a vehicle history report prove that a car has no problems?

No. A history report is useful evidence, but it may not contain every event or reveal the car’s current mechanical condition. The FTC says a vehicle history report is not a substitute for an independent inspection. Ask a mechanic you choose to inspect the vehicle and explain any concerns before you buy.

Check for open safety recalls using the VIN at NHTSA. NHTSA explains that its lookup may not show recalls that have already been repaired, some recently announced recalls before all affected VINs are identified, or certain older or specialized recalls. If the result raises a question, contact the manufacturer or a dealer to confirm the vehicle’s current recall status.

Sources: [FTC used-car guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-used-car-dealer) and [NHTSA recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls).

### What should I do before paying a deposit?

Get the VIN, verify the seller and the car, ask for the complete out-the-door price, and understand the deposit terms in writing. If possible, inspect the actual vehicle and arrange an independent mechanical inspection before paying. Never assume a listing page or chat message includes every condition of the sale.

Keep a copy of the listing and all written promises. For a dealer purchase, read the Buyers Guide and the sale and financing documents. If the seller changes the price, adds fees, or refuses reasonable checks, step back and compare another vehicle.

Sources: [FTC used-car buying tips](https://consumer.ftc.gov/media/79915) and [FTC online used-car buying guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/07/what-know-when-buying-used-car-online).

## 2. How do used-car sites calculate a “good deal” price?

A “good deal” label is a marketplace’s estimate based on its own comparison data and rules. Different sites may use different listings, vehicle details, markets, and fee assumptions, so their labels can disagree. Treat a rating as a starting point: open the comparable vehicles, confirm the dealer’s full price, and check whether the cars truly match.

### What can affect a listing’s price rating?

Vehicle year, make, model, trim, mileage, condition, equipment, location, and disclosed fees can all affect whether two listings are comparable. A rating may be affected by missing or inaccurate vehicle details. The provider’s own explanation should be read before interpreting the badge.

For example, TrueCar says it considers factors such as mileage, location, options, and condition when creating a price rating for a used vehicle. Edmunds describes Price Checker and True Market Value tools for comparing pricing. CarGurus describes Deal Ratings and says listings without disclosed fees can receive “No Rating” under its fee-transparency policy. Those descriptions are not interchangeable formulas; each company controls its own method.

Sources: [TrueCar used-car listings](https://www.truecar.com/used-cars-for-sale/), [Edmunds pricing information](https://help.edmunds.com/hc/en-us/articles/12131482576407-We-provide-car-pricing-information), and [CarGurus fee transparency update](https://dealers.cargurus.com/blog/fee-transparency-update).

### Why can two sites give the same car different ratings?

Each site may use a different set of listings and data fields, and may update its estimates on a different schedule. One may have more relevant comparisons for a local market or may handle fees differently. The label alone does not tell you which estimate is right for your transaction.

Check the provider’s explanation, then build your own comparison. Match model year, trim, drivetrain, mileage, condition, location, and included equipment as closely as possible. Confirm whether the advertised price includes required fees and whether the vehicle is still available. Ask the dealer for a written out-the-door figure, including taxes and registration where applicable.

A rating is not an appraisal of the car’s mechanical condition, a guarantee of the dealer’s price, or a promise that you can buy at that amount. If the details do not match or the listing lacks the fee information the tool needs, treat the rating as incomplete.

Sources: [TrueCar price-rating FAQ](https://www.truecar.com/faq/), [Edmunds used-car pricing information](https://help.edmunds.com/hc/en-us/articles/12131482576407-We-provide-car-pricing-information), and [CarGurus fee transparency update](https://dealers.cargurus.com/blog/fee-transparency-update).

### How can I check whether a “good deal” is actually good?

Compare similar vehicles and calculate the total cost to buy and own the car. A lower advertised price may be offset by condition issues, required fees, financing costs, insurance, or maintenance needs. Ask for a written price breakdown and arrange an independent inspection before committing.

Use more than one pricing source if the estimates differ, but do not average their labels. Instead, inspect the comparable listings and note the differences. Verify the VIN, mileage, trim, title status, equipment, warranty, and location. Then request the seller’s complete price, including mandatory fees and any products the dealer proposes to add.

A useful question is not “Which badge is green?” but “What evidence supports this price, and what would I still pay?” That question is more helpful than treating a marketplace estimate as a final answer.

Sources: [CFPB: Factors in a vehicle’s price](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-factors-into-the-price-of-a-car-or-vehicle-en-723/) and [FTC used-car dealer guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-used-car-dealer).

## 3. What does total cost of ownership include for a used car?

Total cost of ownership includes the purchase and financing costs plus the expenses of keeping and using the vehicle. For a realistic comparison, include insurance, fuel or electricity, routine maintenance, repairs, taxes and registration, and likely resale value. Your actual cost depends on your location, driving, loan, insurance quote, and the car’s condition.

### How should I compare financing costs?

Compare loan offers by annual percentage rate, loan term, fees, amount financed, and total payments—not only by monthly payment. A longer term can lower the monthly payment while increasing the total interest paid. The CFPB recommends comparing the total cost of different auto-loan offers and reviewing required disclosures before signing.

Separate the car’s negotiated price from the financing. A payment quote may include a down payment, trade-in value, add-ons, or a longer term. Ask for a written breakdown so you can compare offers on the same assumptions.

Sources: [CFPB: Compare auto-loan offers](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-compare-auto-loan-offers-what-should-i-look-at-besides-the-monthly-payment-en-753/) and [FTC: Financing or Leasing a Car](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/financing-or-leasing-car).

### How do I estimate insurance before buying?

Request a quote for the exact vehicle and your intended coverage before you commit. Insurance premiums vary with the driver, vehicle, location, coverage choices, and insurer’s risk assessment, so a general estimate may not match your actual premium.

Compare quotes using equivalent coverage limits, deductibles, drivers, and vehicle details. If you are comparing several cars, price each one. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners explains that premiums depend on selected coverage and the insurer’s assessment of risk, including factors such as location and driving record.

Source: [NAIC auto insurance overview](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/auto-insurance).

### How should I account for maintenance and repairs?

Use the manufacturer’s maintenance schedule for the exact model and year, then ask an independent mechanic to assess the specific vehicle. Include near-term service items shown by records or inspection, such as tires, brakes, fluids, or repairs the mechanic identifies. Avoid applying one generic maintenance estimate to every used car.

A well-documented car can still need maintenance, while a car with missing records may require additional investigation. Ask for service invoices where available and have the mechanic explain which work is urgent, which is routine, and which is uncertain. For model-level cost estimates, disclose the source and assumptions; do not present a general estimate as a quote for an individual car.

Source: [FTC used-car guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-used-car-dealer).

### Why include depreciation and resale value?

The cost of ownership is not just the cash you spend while driving. A car’s resale value affects how much value you retain when you sell or trade it. For a personal shopping comparison, estimate resale value conservatively and show the assumption; do not promise what a future buyer or dealer will pay.

The IRS discusses depreciation for business-use vehicles, but that tax treatment is not a consumer forecast of a used car’s future market value. For a practical ownership estimate, use current market evidence for comparable vehicles and state the time horizon and condition assumptions. Keep this estimate separate from expenses such as fuel, insurance, and repairs.

Source: [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946).

### What costs should I put in a simple comparison?

Use the same assumptions for each car: purchase price, required fees, down payment, loan terms, insurance quote, expected miles driven, energy or fuel cost, maintenance and repair allowance, registration and taxes, and estimated resale value. Record which amounts are quotes and which are estimates.

A comparison is only useful if the reader can see what has been included. Do not call a monthly figure “total cost” if it excludes financing, insurance, maintenance, or fees. If an input is unknown, mark it unknown and tell the reader how to obtain it. The CFPB advises shoppers to account for the up-front car cost and recurring costs such as interest, insurance, and routine maintenance.

Source: [CFPB: How much can I afford to borrow for a car?](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-much-can-i-afford-to-borrow-for-a-car-or-auto-loan-en-751/).
