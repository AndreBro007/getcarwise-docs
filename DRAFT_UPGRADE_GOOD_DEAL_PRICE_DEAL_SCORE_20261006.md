## Add after the explanation of the Deal Score’s purpose

### How should I read a “good deal” label from a used-car site?

Read it as that provider’s price estimate, based on its own data and rules. TrueCar says its used-car Price Rating compares recent listing prices for similar vehicles in the shopper’s area, while CarGurus says its IMV and Deal Ratings use all-in prices including disclosed fees. Those systems use different methods, so compare their evidence rather than treating the labels as interchangeable. [TrueCar Price Rating FAQ](https://www.truecar.com/faq/); [CarGurus fee-transparency update](https://dealers.cargurus.com/blog/fee-transparency-update).

The Deal Score is GetCarWise’s own tool, not a universal marketplace label. Use the [Deal Score page](https://getcarwise.app/tools/deal-score/) to evaluate an asking price, then inspect the assumptions and comparable vehicles available for that car. A score can help organize a price check; it does not establish the vehicle’s mechanical condition or guarantee that a seller will accept the indicated price.

### Why can two sites rate the same car differently?

Sites can compare different listings, locations, vehicle details, and fees. A price signal may also be incomplete when the listing lacks information needed by that provider. A difference between ratings is a reason to inspect the underlying comparison and ask the seller for the complete price, not a reason to assume one site is correct.

For example, TrueCar says its used-car Price Rating uses recent local listing prices for similar vehicles, and its FAQ says dealer documentation, administrative, and similar processing fees are excluded. CarGurus says undisclosed-fee used listings receive “No Rating” and rank lower in search, while its IMV and Deal Ratings use all-in prices that include disclosed fees. Attribute these descriptions to the companies; they do not make the two ratings directly comparable. [TrueCar FAQ](https://www.truecar.com/faq/); [CarGurus fee-transparency update](https://dealers.cargurus.com/blog/fee-transparency-update).

### What should I match before comparing two asking prices?

Match the details that change what the vehicle is worth: model year, make and model, trim, mileage, condition, equipment, location, title and warranty status, and the fees included in the price. If the vehicles differ on one of these points, note the difference instead of treating their asking prices as equivalent.

The CFPB explains that vehicle price includes more than the base price: options, add-ons, fees, and financing can affect the total paid. TrueCar also identifies mileage, location, options, and condition as factors in its used-car rating. Use listing details to build a fair comparison, then request the seller’s written out-the-door price. [CFPB: What factors into the price of a car?](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-factors-into-the-price-of-a-car-or-vehicle-en-723/); [TrueCar used-car listings](https://www.truecar.com/used-cars-for-sale/).

### Does a strong price rating mean the car is a good one to buy?

No. A price rating answers a pricing question according to the provider’s method. It does not confirm the title, service history, warranty, or mechanical condition of the particular car. The FTC recommends getting a vehicle history report and an independent mechanic’s inspection; its guidance says a history report is not a substitute for that inspection. [FTC: Buying a Used Car From a Dealer](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-used-car-dealer).

Use the [GetCarWise Deal Score](https://getcarwise.app/tools/deal-score/) as one price-screening input. Then verify the VIN and documents, review the seller’s written price and warranty terms, and inspect the car.
